Advanced Nuclear Engineering: A Graduate Reference

The core graduate curriculum for civil nuclear power, from nuclear physics to reactor safety and AI methods, with equations and worked examples.

1. How to use this guide

This guide covers the core graduate curriculum for civil nuclear power, from nuclear physics to reactor safety and AI methods. Work through Sections 2 to 6 in order, because each builds on the last. Sections 7 to 12 can be read in any order.

Prerequisites: calculus through partial differential equations, linear algebra, undergraduate thermodynamics and fluid mechanics, modern physics, and some Python.

Scope: the worked examples use simplified, illustrative numbers to teach method. Real design and licensing calculations require validated codes, evaluated nuclear data, and qualified review.

2. Nuclear physics foundations

Fission releases energy because heavy nuclei are less tightly bound per nucleon than mid-mass fragments. That difference, about 1 MeV per nucleon, yields roughly 200 MeV per fission.

Binding energy

B(A,Z)=[ZmH​+(A−Z)mn​−matom​]c2

Worked example, U-235. With atomic masses in u, the mass defect is 92(1.007825) + 143(1.008665) − 235.043930 = 1.91505 u. That gives B = 1784 MeV, or 7.59 MeV per nucleon.

The semi-empirical mass formula explains the trend through volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing terms:

B≈av​A−as​A2/3−ac​A1/3Z(Z−1)​−aa​A(A−2Z)2​+δ(A,Z)

Radioactive decay

N(t)=N0​e−λt,A=λN,t1/2​=λln2​

For a chain, the Bateman equations track each daughter's production and loss. Burnup and depletion codes solve the same equations at large scale, with added neutron capture and fission terms.

Neutron interactions

A cross section measures the probability of a given reaction. Reaction rate density is the core quantity of reactor physics:

R=Σϕ=Nσϕ
Reaction Symbol Why it matters
Elastic scattering σₛ Slows neutrons, especially on light nuclei
Inelastic scattering σᵢₙ Slows fast neutrons on heavy nuclei
Radiative capture σγ Removes neutrons, breeds Pu-239 from U-238
Fission σf Releases energy and neutrons

Three energy regions shape behaviour. At thermal energies, many absorption cross sections follow a 1/v law. In the resonance region, cross sections spike sharply, and Doppler broadening of those peaks drives fuel temperature feedback. At fast energies, cross sections are smaller and smoother.

Slowing down

In elastic collisions, the average logarithmic energy loss ξ depends only on the target mass. For hydrogen ξ = 1, and for carbon ξ is about 0.158. This is why water moderates in a short distance and graphite needs far more collisions.

ξ=1+1−ααlnα​,α=(A+1A−1​)2

Capture gamma rays return several MeV more, which brings the usable total to about 200 MeV. So 1 W of thermal power needs about 3.1 × 10¹⁰ fissions per second.

3. Neutron transport and diffusion

The Boltzmann transport equation is the exact neutron balance, and diffusion theory is its most useful approximation. Most reactor analysis is a choice of how far to simplify the first toward the second.

The transport equation

Angular flux ψ depends on position, direction, energy, and time, which makes seven independent variables:

Reading left to right, the terms are storage, streaming, and total removal, balanced by in-scatter, fission production, and external source.

From transport to diffusion

Integrating over direction gives a continuity equation. Assuming a nearly isotropic flux gives Fick's law, J = −D∇φ, with D = 1/(3Σₜᵣ). The result is the steady-state one-group diffusion equation:

−∇⋅D∇ϕ+Σa​ϕ=k1​νΣf​ϕ

Diffusion theory loses accuracy near boundaries, near strong absorbers such as control rods, and within a few mean free paths of material interfaces. Those are the places that need transport corrections or homogenised parameters.

Boundary conditions

Flux and current are continuous across material interfaces. At a vacuum boundary, flux extrapolates linearly to zero a distance d ≈ 0.71 λₜᵣ beyond the physical surface.

Criticality of a bare homogeneous reactor

For a uniform bare system, the flux shape satisfies the Helmholtz equation, ∇²φ + B²φ = 0. Criticality requires the material buckling to equal the geometric buckling:

Bm2​=DνΣf​−Σa​​=Bg2​

Worked example. Take illustrative one-group constants of D = 1.0 cm, Σₐ = 0.05 cm⁻¹, and νΣf = 0.06 cm⁻¹.

  1. Infinite-medium multiplication: k∞ = 0.06/0.05 = 1.20.
  2. Diffusion area: L² = 1.0/0.05 = 20 cm².
  3. Material buckling: B² = (0.06 − 0.05)/1.0 = 0.01 cm⁻².
  4. Critical sphere radius: R = π/√0.01 = 31.4 cm, including the extrapolation distance.

The finite system's multiplication factor shows how leakage competes with production:

keff​=1+L2B2k∞​​

Reflectors

A reflector returns leaking neutrons, which reduces the fuel needed and flattens the power distribution. The saving in core size is called reflector savings. Thermal flux often peaks in a water reflector, because fast neutrons leaking from the core slow down there.

4. Reactor statics

A reactor is critical when each neutron generation exactly replaces the last, so k = 1. Statics asks what geometry and composition achieve that, and what the power shape will be.

The six-factor formula

This formula follows one neutron generation through a thermal reactor:

keff​=ηfpεPFNL​PTNL​
Factor Meaning Main design lever
η Fission neutrons per absorption in fuel Enrichment, fuel type
f Share of thermal absorptions in fuel Moderator ratio, poisons
p Share of neutrons escaping resonance capture Lattice pitch, fuel temperature
ε Boost from fast fission Fuel geometry
P_FNL Fast non-leakage probability Core size, reflector
P_TNL Thermal non-leakage probability Core size, reflector

Worked example. For illustrative fresh light-water fuel values, take η = 1.65, ε = 1.02, p = 0.87, and f = 0.90. Then k∞ = 1.32. With P_FNL = 0.97 and P_TNL = 0.98, k_eff = 1.25, before control rods, soluble boron, or depletion. That excess reactivity is what the control systems must hold down and what burnup slowly consumes.

Moderation ratio

The moderator-to-fuel ratio trades resonance escape against thermal utilisation. More water raises p but lowers f, so k∞ has a peak. Power reactors are designed on the under-moderated side of that peak. Heating or voiding the water then reduces moderation and lowers reactivity, which is a stabilising feedback.

Multigroup diffusion

One energy group cannot represent the spectrum, so energy is divided into groups. The eigenvalue equation for group g is:

−∇⋅Dg​∇ϕg​+Σr,g​ϕg​=g′=g∑​Σs,g′→g​ϕg′​+kχg​​g′∑​νΣf,g′​ϕg′​

For two groups in an LWR, all fission neutrons are born fast, and upscatter is neglected. Infinite-medium multiplication then has a closed form:

k∞​=Σa1​+Σs1→2​νΣf1​+νΣf2​Σs1→2​/Σa2​​

The standard numerical solution is outer power iteration on the fission source, wrapped around inner solves for the flux in each group. Convergence depends on the dominance ratio, which approaches 1 in large, loosely coupled cores. Acceleration methods such as coarse-mesh finite difference exist for this reason.

The two-step lattice-to-core method

Production LWR analysis usually splits the problem by scale:

  1. Lattice physics. A high-fidelity transport code solves a single fuel assembly with many energy groups.
  2. Homogenisation. The results collapse into few-group constants, tabulated against burnup, temperature, density, and boron.
  3. Core simulation. A nodal diffusion code solves the whole core using those constants.
  4. Pin power reconstruction. Assembly form functions recover the power in each rod.

Fully coupled whole-core transport is increasingly practical on large computers, but the two-step path remains the workhorse for routine reload design.

Self-shielding and resonance treatment

Inside a fuel pellet, the flux dips sharply at resonance energies, so a plain cross-section average would overstate absorption. Multigroup libraries handle this with self-shielding methods. Common examples are the Bondarenko method and subgroup methods.

Power peaking

Fuel limits are set by the hottest location, not the average. Designers track the ratio of peak to average power, called the peaking factor. Tools for flattening power include fuel loading patterns, burnable absorbers such as gadolinia, and reflectors.

5. Reactor kinetics and dynamics

Reactors are controllable because about 0.65% of U-235 fission neutrons are delayed by seconds to minutes. That small fraction stretches the effective response from microseconds to a timescale that control systems can follow.

Point kinetics

If the flux shape stays fixed, neutron population n(t) and six delayed precursor groups Cᵢ follow:

dtdn​=Λρ−β​n+i=1∑6​λi​Ci​,dtdCi​​=Λβi​​n−λi​Ci​

Reactivity is often quoted in dollars, where $1 equals β. At ρ = $1 the reactor is prompt critical, meaning prompt neutrons alone sustain the chain. Normal operation and design limits stay well below that point.

The inhour equation links a constant reactivity to the stable reactor period T:

ρ=TΛ​+i=1∑6​1+λi​Tβi​​

Prompt jump

For a small step in reactivity, power jumps quickly and then rises on a slow stable period. With one effective precursor group:

n0​n+​​≈β−ρβ​,T≈λρβ−ρ​

Worked example. Take β = 0.0065, λ = 0.08 s⁻¹, and a step of ρ = 0.001 (100 pcm).

  1. Prompt jump: 0.0065/0.0055 = 1.18, so power rises 18% almost at once.
  2. Stable period: 0.0055/(0.08 × 0.001) = 69 s.

Reactivity feedback

Feedback couples neutronics to temperature and density. It is the main reason an LWR is inherently self-limiting.

Mechanism Physical cause Timescale Sign in a typical LWR
Doppler (fuel temperature) Resonance broadening raises U-238 capture Near-immediate Negative
Moderator temperature Lower water density reduces moderation Seconds Negative in normal operation
Void Steam displaces moderator Seconds Negative
Soluble boron Hotter, less dense water carries less boron Seconds Makes the moderator coefficient less negative

Design choices can change these signs. Large sodium-cooled fast reactors, for example, can have a positive void effect in some regions, which designers must offset through geometry and other feedbacks.

Fission product poisons

Xe-135 has an absorption cross section near 2.6 million barns, among the largest known for any nuclide. Most of it forms from I-135 decay.

dtdI​=γI​Σf​ϕ−λI​I,dtdX​=γX​Σf​ϕ+λI​I−λX​X−σaX​ϕX

I-135 has a half-life of about 6.6 hours and Xe-135 about 9.1 hours. Three operational effects follow:

  • Equilibrium. Xenon settles within about two days of steady full power.
  • Post-shutdown peak. Xenon burnout stops but iodine keeps decaying, so xenon peaks several hours after shutdown. This can delay restart.
  • Spatial oscillation. In large cores, axial xenon swings can develop over roughly a day and need active management.

Sm-149 is a stable poison from Pm-149 decay. It reaches equilibrium over weeks and stays after shutdown until burned out at restart.

Decay heat

Fission products keep producing heat after shutdown, which is why cooling remains a safety function. The Way-Wigner approximation gives a useful estimate, with operating time T₀ in seconds:

P0​P(t)​≈0.0622[t−0.2−(t+T0​)−0.2]

For one year of prior operation, this gives about 6.0% of full power at 1 second, 1.0% at 1 hour, and 0.44% at 1 day. A 3000 MWt core would still produce about 13 MW a day after shutdown. Licensing analyses use standards such as ANS-5.1 instead.

Depletion and burnup

As fuel burns, U-235 falls, Pu-239 builds up, and fission products accumulate. Plutonium fission supplies a large share of power late in fuel life. The depletion equations form a large matrix system:

dtdN​=A(ϕ)N

Codes solve this with matrix exponential methods such as CRAM, and they alternate with flux solves. Burnup is measured in MWd per kg of heavy metal, and fissioning about 1 g of U-235 yields about 1 MWd.

6. Thermal hydraulics

Thermal limits, not neutronics, usually cap a reactor's power. The job of thermal hydraulics is to move heat from fuel to coolant while keeping fuel and cladding within their limits.

Core energy balance

Q˙​=m˙cp​(Tout​−Tin​)

For example, 3000 MWt with 15,000 kg/s of coolant at cₚ = 5.5 kJ/kg·K gives a coolant temperature rise of about 36 K.

Temperature through a fuel rod

For a cylindrical pellet with uniform heating, the centre-to-surface temperature drop depends only on linear heat rate and conductivity. It does not depend on pellet radius:

Tcl​−Ts​=4πkf​q′​

The gap, cladding, and coolant film add series resistances:

ΔTgap​=2πrf​hg​q′​,ΔTclad​=2πrf​kc​q′tc​​,ΔTfilm​=2πro​hq′​

Worked example. Take q′ = 20 kW/m and a local coolant temperature of 300 °C. These illustrative inputs are near a peak rod location in a PWR.

Layer Inputs Temperature rise (K) Temperature on inner side (°C)
Coolant film h = 30,000 W/m²·K, rₒ = 4.67 mm 23 323
Cladding k = 17 W/m·K, t = 0.57 mm 26 349
Gap h = 5,000 W/m²·K, r = 4.1 mm 155 504
Fuel pellet k = 3 W/m·K 531 1035

The fuel carries most of the temperature drop because UO₂ is a poor conductor. Its conductivity also falls with burnup, and the gap closes as the pellet swells, so real calculations vary both over fuel life.

Convection

For turbulent single-phase flow in a channel, the Dittus-Boelter correlation is the usual first estimate:

Nu=khDe​​=0.023Re0.8Pr0.4

Rod bundles need correlations corrected for pitch-to-diameter ratio. Liquid metals have very low Prandtl numbers, so they need separate correlations with conduction-dominated behaviour.

Boiling and two-phase flow

The boiling curve shows how heat flux varies with wall superheat. It runs through natural convection, nucleate boiling, the critical heat flux, transition boiling, and film boiling. Nucleate boiling removes heat very efficiently. Past the critical heat flux, a vapour blanket forms and the wall temperature can jump by hundreds of degrees.

Flow quality and void fraction are linked through the slip ratio S:

Natural circulation and analysis tools

Natural circulation drives flow by density differences alone, which is central to many passive safety systems. Flow rises with the thermal driving head and falls with loop friction.

Analysis tools operate at three scales: system codes such as RELAP5 and TRACE, subchannel codes such as COBRA-TF and VIPRE, and computational fluid dynamics for local detail.

7. Nuclear materials and fuel performance

Radiation, heat, and corrosion together set how long reactor components last. Fuel lifetime and vessel lifetime are often the practical limits on plant economics.

Radiation damage

A fast neutron knocks an atom from its lattice site, and that atom displaces others in a damage cascade. Damage is measured in displacements per atom (dpa), which allows comparison across neutron spectra. The NRT model estimates displacements from damage energy T_d and threshold energy E_d:

Nd​=2Ed​0.8Td​​
Effect Mechanism Typical conditions Consequence
Hardening and embrittlement Defect clusters and solute precipitates pin dislocations Low-temperature irradiation of steels Higher strength, lower toughness
Void swelling Vacancies gather into voids Roughly 0.3 to 0.6 of melting temperature, high dpa Dimensional change, distortion
Irradiation creep Stress-biased defect absorption Stress with irradiation Deformation below thermal creep range
Helium embrittlement (n,α) helium gathers at grain boundaries High temperature Loss of ductility
Radiation-induced segregation Solute flows with defects to sinks Grain boundaries in stainless steels Susceptibility to cracking

Pressure vessel embrittlement

The reactor pressure vessel is the key component that cannot practically be replaced. Neutron exposure raises its ductile-to-brittle transition temperature. Copper, nickel, and phosphorus content all increase sensitivity, which is why surveillance capsules track the shift throughout plant life. The main concern is pressurised thermal shock, where cold water meets a hot, pressurised, embrittled wall.

Fuel behaviour

UO₂ changes substantially during its time in the core:

  • Cracking and relocation. Thermal stress cracks pellets during the first rise to power.
  • Densification and swelling. Pellets first shrink slightly, then swell as fission products build up.
  • Fission gas release. Xenon and krypton migrate to the gap, reducing gap conductance and raising internal pressure.
  • High burnup structure. A fine-grained, porous rim forms at the pellet edge at high local burnup.
  • Pellet-cladding interaction. Swollen pellets press on the cladding, and iodine can add stress corrosion cracking during power ramps.

Cladding

Zirconium alloys are used because of their low neutron absorption and good corrosion resistance. Their main degradation modes are waterside corrosion and hydride embrittlement. At high accident temperatures, zirconium also reacts exothermically with steam:

Zr+2H2​O→ZrO2​+2H2​+heat

This reaction added heat and generated the hydrogen behind the explosions at Fukushima Daiichi. It was a main driver of later fuel research.

Accident tolerant fuel

Accident tolerant fuels aim to give operators more time during a loss of cooling, and they do so by reducing oxidation and hydrogen generation.

8. The nuclear fuel cycle

The fuel cycle runs from uranium mining to final waste disposal, and its back end is now reaching a major milestone. Finland granted the world's first operating licence for a permanent spent fuel repository, announced in October 2026.

Stages

  1. Mining and milling. Ore becomes uranium concentrate, known as yellowcake.
  2. Conversion. Concentrate becomes UF₆ for enrichment.
  3. Enrichment. U-235 content rises from 0.711% to about 3 to 5% for LWR fuel.
  4. Fabrication. UF₆ becomes UO₂ pellets, loaded into cladding and assemblies.
  5. Irradiation. Fuel typically spends several years in the core.
  6. Interim storage. Spent fuel cools in pools, then moves to dry casks.
  7. Recycling or disposal. Spent fuel is either reprocessed or sent to a geological repository.

Enrichment accounting

Mass balance gives the natural uranium feed F needed per unit of product P, with tails T:

PF​=xf​−xt​xp​−xt​​,T=F−P

Separative work is the standard measure of enrichment effort, and it sets a large part of fuel cost:

SWU=PV(xp​)+TV(xt​)−FV(xf​),V(x)=(2x−1)ln1−xx​

Worked example. Produce 1 kg of 4.5% LWR fuel from natural feed (0.711%), with 0.25% tails.

  1. Feed: (0.045 − 0.0025)/(0.00711 − 0.0025) = 9.22 kg natural uranium.
  2. Tails: 9.22 − 1 = 8.22 kg.
  3. Separative work: about 6.87 SWU.

Fuel cycle economics trade uranium against enrichment. Lower tails assay saves uranium but needs more separative work, so the best tails value moves with their relative prices.

Spent LWR fuel is roughly 95% uranium, 1% plutonium, and 4% fission products and minor actinides. Commercial reprocessing has used the PUREX process, and research continues into advanced alternatives.

Waste management

Spent fuel heat comes mostly from short-lived fission products at first, then from Cs-137 and Sr-90 over decades, and later from actinides. Heat load therefore limits how densely waste can be placed in a repository.

Waste class Description Disposal approach
Very low and low level Lightly contaminated items and materials Near-surface facilities
Intermediate level Resins, reactor internals Intermediate depth facilities
High level Spent fuel or vitrified reprocessing waste Deep geological repository

Deep geological disposal relies on multiple barriers. In Finland's KBS-3 design, fuel sits in copper canisters surrounded by bentonite clay, deep in crystalline bedrock. Safety cases model groundwater flow, corrosion, and radionuclide transport over hundreds of thousands of years.

Safeguards and security

Safeguards verify that civil nuclear material stays in peaceful use. Under the Non-Proliferation Treaty, the IAEA applies material accounting, inspections, and containment and surveillance. National regulators separately set physical protection requirements. Design for these needs early in a project is called safeguards-by-design.

9. Radiation shielding, detection, and dosimetry

Radiation protection rests on three controls: less time, more distance, and more shielding. Shielding calculations estimate how far each radiation type penetrates, while detectors and dosimeters verify the result.

Gamma shielding

For a narrow beam, uncollided intensity falls exponentially with the linear attenuation coefficient μ. Scattered photons add to the result, so real calculations include a buildup factor B:

I(x)=B(μx)I0​e−μx,ϕ(r)=4πr2SBe−μr​

Worked example. Take an illustrative μ = 0.5 cm⁻¹ and a 10 cm slab. The half-value layer is ln 2/μ = 1.39 cm. The uncollided fraction is e⁻⁵ = 0.67%. The true dose would be higher once buildup is included, so ignoring it is non-conservative.

Neutron shielding

Neutron shields usually work in three steps:

  1. Slow down. Hydrogen-rich materials such as water, polyethylene, and concrete moderate fast neutrons. Heavy elements help through inelastic scatter.
  2. Absorb. Boron or cadmium captures the slowed neutrons.
  3. Stop secondary gamma rays. Capture produces gamma rays, so dense material such as steel or lead is needed too.

The usual tools are discrete ordinates codes and Monte Carlo codes such as MCNP. Deep-penetration Monte Carlo problems need variance reduction, because few particles reach the region of interest. Hybrid methods such as CADIS use a deterministic adjoint solution to guide the sampling.

Counting is Poisson distributed, so the standard deviation of N counts is √N. Collecting 10,000 counts gives 1% relative uncertainty. Reactor instrumentation spans source, intermediate, and power ranges, because flux spans about ten orders of magnitude from shutdown to full power.

The radiation weighting factor is 1 for photons and electrons, 20 for alpha particles, and energy dependent for neutrons, per ICRP 103.

Biological effects and limits

High doses cause tissue reactions above a threshold, such as acute radiation syndrome. Stochastic effects such as cancer have a probability that scales with dose. Regulators generally adopt the linear no-threshold model for protection, although its accuracy at low doses remains debated.

ICRP reference value Effective dose
Occupational limit 20 mSv/year, averaged over 5 years, with no single year above 50 mSv
Public limit 1 mSv/year above background
Worldwide average natural background about 2.4 mSv/year

The ALARA principle requires doses to be kept as low as reasonably achievable, even below these limits.

10. Reactor safety, risk, and licensing

The IAEA safety standards are the international baseline, and national regulators build their rules on them to differing degrees. Every standard serves three fundamental safety functions: control reactivity, remove heat from the fuel, and confine radioactive material.

The IAEA safety standards

The standards form a three-level hierarchy. They are not binding by themselves, but countries adopt them into national law, and the IAEA uses them in peer reviews of regulators and plants.

Level Purpose Key examples
Safety Fundamentals Principles behind all other standards SF-1, Fundamental Safety Principles
Safety Requirements What must be achieved, written as "shall" SSR-2/1 (Rev. 1), Design of nuclear power plants; SSR-2/2, Commissioning and operation; GSR Part 4, Safety assessment; SSR-1, Site evaluation
Safety Guides How to meet the requirements, written as "should" SSG-2, Deterministic safety analysis; SSG-3 and SSG-4, Level 1 and Level 2 probabilistic safety assessment

SSR-2/1 (Rev. 1) dates from 2016. A full revision is in preparation, with publication targeted for 2031. The Convention on Nuclear Safety adds a treaty layer, under which member countries report on their safety programmes and review each other.

The ten fundamental safety principles

SF-1 sets ten principles that apply to every country and facility:

  1. The licensee holds prime responsibility for safety.
  2. Government sets up an effective, independent legal and regulatory framework.
  3. Leadership and management build a strong safety culture.
  4. Facilities that give rise to radiation risk must yield an overall benefit.
  5. Protection is optimised to the highest level reasonably achievable.
  6. Risk to any individual is limited.
  7. Present and future generations and the environment are protected.
  8. All practical efforts are made to prevent and mitigate accidents.
  9. Arrangements are in place for emergency preparedness and response.
  10. Protective actions reduce existing or unregulated radiation risks.

Defense in depth

The IAEA framework uses five levels, so that a failure at one level is caught by the next:

Level Objective Examples
1 Prevent abnormal operation Conservative design, quality management
2 Control abnormal operation Control and limiting systems
3 Control design-basis accidents Emergency core cooling, reactor protection
4 Manage severe accidents Containment protection, accident management
5 Mitigate off-site consequences Emergency planning

Physical barriers work in parallel with these levels: the fuel matrix, cladding, reactor coolant boundary, and containment.

Plant states

SSR-2/1 sorts every condition a plant can be in by how likely it is and how severe:

Plant state Meaning Examples
Normal operation Operation within specified limits Power operation, refuelling
Anticipated operational occurrence Expected at least once in the plant's life Turbine trip, loss of a feedwater pump
Design-basis accident Postulated accident the design must handle with limited damage Pipe break, control rod ejection
Design extension condition, no significant fuel damage Beyond the design basis, such as multiple failures Station blackout, failure of safety injection
Design extension condition, with core melting Severe accident the design must still mitigate Core damage with containment intact

Event sequences that could cause an early or large radioactive release must be "practically eliminated". That means showing they are physically impossible or extremely unlikely with high confidence.

Design-basis events

Event Description Main concern
Large-break LOCA Major pipe break, rapid depressurisation Peak cladding temperature during reflood
Small-break LOCA Slower coolant loss Long-term core uncovery, operator action
Reactivity-initiated accident Control rod ejection or drop Rapid fuel enthalpy rise
Loss of flow Pump trip or seizure Thermal margin to DNB
Anticipated transient without scram Shutdown system fails after a transient Pressure and power, relies on feedback
Station blackout Loss of all AC power Long-term decay heat removal

Each country sets its own numerical acceptance criteria. For example, US rules limit peak cladding temperature to 1204 °C and local oxidation to 17% for a LOCA, and many other regulators use similar values. A large LOCA runs through blowdown, refill, reflood, and long-term cooling.

Lessons from major accidents

Accident INES level Main cause Lasting lesson
Three Mile Island, 1979 5 Stuck valve, misread instruments, operator action Human factors, training, control room design
Chernobyl, 1986 7 Unstable design at low power, positive void effect, procedure violations Inherent feedback design, safety culture
Fukushima Daiichi, 2011 7 Tsunami caused extended station blackout External hazards, diverse backup power, hydrogen control

The International Nuclear and Radiological Event Scale (INES) rates events from 0, with no safety significance, to 7, a major accident. It gives the public a consistent way to judge severity across countries.

Severe accident phenomena

If cooling fails for long enough, the core may melt. Important phenomena include zirconium oxidation and hydrogen generation, core relocation, vessel failure, and interaction between molten core and concrete. Mitigation options include hydrogen recombiners, filtered venting, in-vessel melt retention, and core catchers. Integrated codes such as MELCOR, MAAP, and ASTEC model these sequences.

Probabilistic safety assessment

Probabilistic safety assessment (PSA), also called PRA, asks what can go wrong, how likely it is, and what the consequences would be. It uses event trees for sequences and fault trees for system failures.

PSA level Calculates Key metric
Level 1 Core damage sequences Core damage frequency (CDF)
Level 2 Containment response and release Large or large early release frequency
Level 3 Off-site health and economic effects Public risk

The frequency of a sequence is its initiating event frequency multiplied by each system failure probability along the path:

fseq​=fIE​i∏​Pi​

Worked example. Take an illustrative initiating event at 10⁻² per year, an injection failure of 10⁻³, and a recovery failure of 0.1. The sequence frequency is 10⁻⁶ per reactor-year.

The IAEA's INSAG-12 report suggested targets of CDF below 10⁻⁴ per reactor-year for existing plants and 10⁻⁵ for new plants. For large early releases, the targets are ten times lower. National regulators set their own targets, often close to these values.

Importance measures such as Fussell-Vesely and risk achievement worth then rank the components that contribute most. Common-cause failures and human reliability are often the largest sources of uncertainty.

National licensing

Every country licenses its own plants, so a design approved in one country must still be licensed in each country that builds it. The approaches below show the range.

Approach Example regulator Main features
Prescriptive, staged licences US Nuclear Regulatory Commission Construction and operating licences, or a combined licence. An optional, risk-informed, technology-inclusive framework, 10 CFR Part 53, took effect on April 29, 2026
Goal-setting, licensee proves safety UK Office for Nuclear Regulation Generic design assessment before a site licence; risks reduced "as low as reasonably practicable"
Staged licences with performance focus Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission Separate licences to prepare a site, construct, and operate
Adopted from IAEA standards Many newcomer countries National law built directly on IAEA requirements, often with support from an experienced regulator

Wherever the plant is, a safety case generally combines deterministic analysis with PSA, plus analysis of siting, security, and emergency planning. Regulators are also cooperating more on reviews of shared designs, especially SMRs, to avoid repeating the same work in every country.

TRISO particles surround a fuel kernel with carbon and silicon carbide layers. Each particle acts as a tiny containment and tolerates very high temperatures.

Small modular reactors and microreactors

The IAEA usually defines SMRs as up to about 300 MWe. Their appeal is factory fabrication, lower upfront cost, and passive safety. Their main economic risk is losing economies of scale before learning effects emerge. Microreactors are smaller still, typically 20 MWe or below, and they target remote sites and specialised loads.

Fusion basics

Fusion has a different set of engineering challenges. The leading fuel, deuterium-tritium, releases 17.6 MeV per reaction, with 14.1 MeV carried by a fast neutron:

D+T→4He (3.5 MeV)+n (14.1 MeV)

The Lawson criterion sets the required combination of density, temperature, and confinement time. For D-T, the triple product n T τ must reach about 3 × 10²¹ keV·s/m³.

Approach Examples How it confines plasma
Magnetic Tokamaks such as ITER, stellarators such as Wendelstein 7-X Strong magnetic fields
Inertial National Ignition Facility Laser-driven implosion

The National Ignition Facility first exceeded laser energy with fusion energy in December 2022. For a power plant, remaining problems include breeding tritium in a lithium blanket, first-wall materials under neutron damage, and heat exhaust.

12. Computational methods and AI

Modern analysis couples physics solvers that were once run separately, and AI can make these expensive calculations faster to explore. AI supplements validated physics codes for now, and it does not replace them in safety decisions.

Multiphysics coupling

Each solver passes results to the next, and the loop repeats until the fields converge. An AI surrogate sits outside that loop, trained on its results.

Monte Carlo in brief

Monte Carlo simulates individual neutron histories by sampling from known probability distributions. Its uncertainty falls with the square root of the number of histories N, so halving the error takes four times the histories:

σxˉ​∝N​1​

Before tallying, criticality runs must discard inactive batches until the fission source converges. Shannon entropy of the source is a standard diagnostic for this.

A first numerical exercise

This Python script solves one-group point kinetics. It reproduces the Section 5 example, printing n(0.1 s) = 1.182 and a stable period of 68.9 s. Stiff equations like these need an implicit solver.

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

beta, lam, Lam, rho = 0.0065, 0.08, 1e-4, 0.001

def rhs(t, y):
    n, c = y
    return [(rho - beta) / Lam * n + lam * c, beta / Lam * n - lam * c]

y0 = [1.0, beta / (Lam * lam)]
sol = solve_ivp(rhs, (0, 200), y0, method="Radau",
                dense_output=True, rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-10)
n = lambda t: sol.sol(t)[0]
period = 100 / np.log(n(200) / n(100))
print(f"n(0.1 s) = {n(0.1):.3f}, stable period = {period:.1f} s")

Digital twins

A digital twin is a model that stays synchronised with a physical asset using live data. Useful twins pair a fast surrogate with data assimilation, such as a Kalman filter, and they report their uncertainty. Early uses are advisory ones, such as monitoring, maintenance planning, and operator support.

Verification, validation, and uncertainty

Credibility matters more than accuracy on a test set. These questions should be answered before relying on any model:

  1. Verification. Does the code solve its equations correctly?
  2. Validation. Does it match measured data under relevant conditions?
  3. Uncertainty quantification. How much do inputs, nuclear data, and model form affect the answer?
  4. Domain of applicability. Does the model recognise and flag when an input is outside its training data?
  5. Safety classification. Would a wrong output affect a safety function, or only a recommendation?

For AI tools, treat regulated uses conservatively: keep a human reviewer, preserve traceability to sources, and confirm with a qualified physics code.
